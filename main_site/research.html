Research

Our research brings economics and AI together to study decisions, interactions, and economic change. The directions and collaborations below connect these questions to new methods, data, and evidence.

AI for Public Deliberation — SBS D Forum 2026

This collaboration will be presented at SBS D Forum 2026 on November 5, 2026, in the session “We Let AI Agents Deliberate.”

This collaboration explores how AI simulations can help us understand public deliberation and design better discussions. econai@kaist developed 10,000 AI personas built on NVIDIA’s Nemotron personas and grounded in data from the Korea Social Integration Survey, then ran their deliberation sessions. By varying group composition, discussion rules, and AI models, the project examines how opinions and arguments evolve.

This project asks how AI can strengthen, rather than erode, our ability to think for ourselves. As one answer, we show how simulated deliberation among AI agents can broaden the way we think and debate, as a complement to deliberation among people, not a substitute for it. Using AI to sharpen our questions and work through a range of perspectives and arguments can prepare us for richer conversations with others. It is in those conversations, with people whose lives and experiences differ from our own, that we weigh one another’s reasons, form our own judgments, and truly exercise independent thought.

econai@kaist researchers: Jihee Kim, Chaemin Jang, Junsik Min, and Jaewoo Choi.

Collaborators: Future Publics Lab (Prof. Nuri Kim, KAIST) and KIXLAB (Prof. Juho Kim, KAIST).

AI 공론장 시뮬레이션 — SBS D포럼 2026

이 공동연구는 2026년 11월 5일 SBS D포럼 2026의 「AI끼리 토론시켜 보았다」 세션에서 소개될 예정입니다.

AI 시뮬레이션을 통해 시민 공론장을 이해하고 더 나은 토론을 설계할 수 있는지 탐구하는 공동연구입니다. econai@kaist는 NVIDIA의 Nemotron 페르소나에 한국행정연구원의 사회통합실태조사 자료를 접목해 가상 시민 1만 명을 만들고, 이들이 참여하는 AI 토론 실험을 수행했습니다. 참여자 구성, 토론 규칙, AI 모델을 바꾸면서 의견과 논거가 어떻게 달라지는지 살펴봅니다.

이 연구는 AI가 우리의 생각주권, 즉 스스로 생각하고 판단하는 힘을 어떻게 뒷받침할 수 있을지 묻습니다. 그 답의 하나로, AI 공론장이 사람의 공론장을 대신하기보다 우리의 사고와 토론의 폭을 넓히는 데 기여할 수 있음을 보여주고자 합니다. AI와 함께 질문을 다듬고 다양한 관점과 논거를 살피는 일은 그 출발점이 될 수 있습니다. 이러한 준비를 바탕으로 서로 다른 삶의 경험을 가진 사람들과 생각을 나누고, 각자의 이유를 함께 따져 보며 자신의 판단을 만들어 갈 때 생각주권을 실현할 수 있다는 메시지를 전하고자 합니다.

econai@kaist 참여 연구진: 김지희, 장채민, 민준식, 최재우.

공동연구: Future Publics Lab(김누리 교수, KAIST), KIXLAB(김주호 교수, KAIST).

Social Simulation with AI Agents

This collaboration develops a shared framework for studying human decisions and collective behavior with AI agents, spanning public deliberation and economic decision-making.

Within this collaboration, econai@kaist develops and evaluates economic simulations that connect individual decisions, belief formation, and strategic interaction to market dynamics and macroeconomic outcomes. By varying information, incentives, and institutional rules, we investigate how differences across agents and their interactions shape aggregate responses to economic shocks and policy changes. A central goal is to ground these simulations in human evidence, assessing their economic alignment and their potential to inform economic research and policy.

Collaborators: Future Publics Lab (Prof. Nuri Kim, KAIST), Collaborative Social Technologies Lab (Prof. Joseph Seering, KAIST), and Data Mining Lab (Prof. Jae-Gil Lee, KAIST).

Modeling and Predicting Economic Behavior

We use observed human behavior to model and predict how people make decisions and interact. By connecting these models to economic simulations, we aim to understand how differences in beliefs, preferences, and circumstances shape market dynamics and aggregate responses to economic shocks and policy changes.

A central aim is economic alignment: developing and evaluating AI models and agents that faithfully represent human preferences and responses to incentives across diverse individuals. This involves assessing both individual behavior and the aggregate outcomes that emerge through interactions.

Current directions

  • Modeling behavior in Polymarket: Learning from market participation and trading decisions to develop models of individual behavior, with applications to prediction and economic simulation.

  • AI-assisted surveys: Investigating when AI-generated responses can complement human survey data and improve population estimates, while retaining human evidence as the foundation for evaluation.

AI Knowledge, Reasoning, and Behavior

We study how AI learns about the world and uses what it has learned to respond to new questions. This connects foundational research on model behavior with evaluations of how reliably AI can support economic and social research.

Knowledge and Judgment in Language Models

We examine why a model’s knowledge or internal judgment may fail to appear in its responses. By studying how training and context shape this connection, we seek to understand the gap between a model’s underlying capabilities and its observable behavior.

Economic Reasoning and Bias

Economic relationships depend on context: an intervention that works in one setting may have different effects under different institutions, market conditions, or populations. We investigate whether AI can recognize these differences and reason from the relevant evidence.

Our EconCausal benchmark brings together over 10,000 causal relationships from empirical studies to evaluate how AI reasons about economic cause and effect across different contexts. Related work examines how ideological biases shape this reasoning.

EconCausal collaborators include: Prof. Sangyoon Park (HKUST).

Measuring Economic Change with AI

We use AI, satellite imagery, and geospatial data to measure economic change where conventional statistics are incomplete or slow to capture it. By connecting these observations with economic knowledge, we aim to make local and regional changes more visible and provide evidence for economic research and public decisions.

Economic Measurement with Geospatial AI

We develop methods for estimating economic indicators and tracking change across places and over time. Work including GeoSEE and GeoChange combines geospatial observations with AI to study local development and regional disparities, including in North Korea, where conventional economic data are limited. These methods also provide a foundation for studying specific sectors, including agriculture.

Collaborator: Data Science for Humanity Group (Prof. Meeyoung Cha, MPI-SP and KAIST).

Agricultural Crop Mapping

Working with South Korea’s Ministry of Unification and collaborators at Stanford, we use satellite imagery and AI to map crop types and their distribution in North Korea. Establishing what is grown where provides a foundation for understanding agricultural production in places where reliable statistics are scarce.

Our longer-term goal is to combine these crop maps with information on crop yields, weather, and growing conditions to improve agricultural production estimates. This would support assessments of food availability and vulnerability to production shocks. Building on that evidence, we aim to study how climate change affects agricultural productivity and food security, and how adaptation can strengthen the resilience of agricultural systems.

Collaborators: Lobell Lab (Prof. David Lobell, Stanford University) and Burney Research Group (Prof. Jennifer Burney, Stanford University).
